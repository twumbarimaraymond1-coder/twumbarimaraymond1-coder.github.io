The brief

Torpey wants an automated lab rig that produces repeatable sweet spot maps of sports equipment, plus a validated protocol to keep using it. The industrial supervisor is Dr Lauren Holmes. The brief says the system should be adaptable to a range of equipment, but the first target is almost certainly the hurley bás (next section).

The four objectives map onto four subsystems:

ObjectiveWhat the brief asksSubsystem
OB1A repeatable impact delivery mechanism for lab testingImpactor
OB2Automated positioning across many impact locations, in a standardised wayXY stage and motion controller
OB3Instrumentation and DAQ that quantify impact response as performance metricsSensors and data acquisition
OB4Software that automates testing, draws the maps and validates repeatability and accuracyPython control and analysis

Deliverables are the rig, the DAQ and analysis code, maps for selected products, a validation report and a testing protocol for future use. Torpey provides the equipment to test, its existing testing knowledge, and resources for manufacture "if appropriately requested".

Sponsor and sport context

Torpey makes hurleys, so the first maps will almost certainly be of the bás. The bás is wide and flat, so the rig needs a genuine 2D map rather than the single line used on a bat.

  • Company. A family firm in Sixmilebridge, Co. Clare, founded in 1981 (About Torpey, The42, 2020). Seán Torpey is Managing Director, and the company won Best Sports Business at the Irish Sport Industry Awards in May 2026 (Clare Echo). It plans to expand into other sports markets in Q3/Q4 2026 (Sport for Business), which may explain the "range of equipment" wording.
  • Products. The range includes Bambú hurleys (Speed, Strike, Sleek, Goalkeeper, Kids/Youth), Ash 6-Star hurleys, a hurling glove, grips and sliotars. Bambú is a natural fibre composite of bamboo particles in a patented matrix, not a laminate (FAQs). Bambú Speed at 32–34 in comes in Light 550 g, Medium 585 g and Heavy 620 g; shorter sizes are lighter.
  • Sweet spot claims. Bambú Speed is sold on a "refined sweet spot", and the FAQ says scientific testing showed an increased sweet spot. The rig could put numbers behind those claims. The Strike page makes no sweet spot claim.
  • Loughborough link. Loughborough's Sports Technology Institute (STI) was the testing site for Bambú: it fired balls at the stick, ran stick-on-stick impacts and benchmarked against ash (NCUB, 2020). The Irish patent was granted in July 2022 (Irish Examiner, 2023). No published method shows how the sweet spot was measured, so ask Torpey for the STI reports.
  • Parallel work. DCU and the GAA advertised a PhD in 2023 to build hurley and sliotar test systems, including a robot arm that mimics the swing (DCU, 2023). Position this project as spatial mapping across the bás, and check for 2024–26 outputs before describing where that work stands.

Defining the sweet spot

There is no single sweet spot, and the definition picked decides the hardware, so agree it with Torpey before concept generation. Brody (1981) set out several definitions for tennis rackets. Brody (1986) analysed three for bats, and Cross (1998) showed they sit in different places.

DefinitionWhat it meansKey sourcesHow it is measuredSensitive to how the implement is held?
Maximum rebound (COR, ACOR, eA)The point giving the fastest ball exit speed relative to inbound speedBrody 1986; Nathan 2000, 2003; Cross 2014Ball cannon or launcher, plus light gates, a high-speed camera or radarLittle, for bat barrel impacts; untested on a wide bás
Vibration nodeAn impact at the node of the first bending mode does not excite that mode, so there is least stingCross 1998; RussellRoving impact hammer plus accelerometer (experimental modal analysis), free-free supportYes: mode shapes depend on the support
Centre of percussion (COP)The impact point giving zero reaction impulse at a chosen pivotBrody 1986; Cross 2004; ASTM F2398Swing the implement as a pendulum on a pivot to get its moment of inertia and COPDefined by the pivot, so not a fixed property
Minimum handle vibrationThe impact point that minimises vibration energy at the handleBlanes et al. 2023Repeatable striker plus handle accelerometerYes
  • The felt sweet spot is a zone. Cross (1998) found it is a narrow zone between the node and the COP, where the least energy reaches the handle. With a hand-held implement the rotation axis passes through the hand, so the node usually feels best (Cross 2004).
  • Collision efficiency. Nathan (2003) formalised the model-independent collision efficiency eA, building on Hester and Koenig (1993). Here e is the COR and r is the ball mass divided by the implement's effective mass at the impact point:
eA=e − r1 + r
  • Support matters less for rebound. For bat barrel impacts, eA and exit speed barely depend on how the handle is held, because the ball leaves before the bending wave returns from the handle (Nathan 2000, 2003). That justifies a clamped or pivoted rig for a rebound map, but check it on the bás.
  • Impact speed matters. At game speeds, exit speed falls sharply away from a small region (Nathan 2000), and the best point moves with speed. Allen, Choppin and Knudson recommend test conditions that match real strokes, so a low-speed map may rank products differently from a game-speed one.

A sensible default is to make rebound (ACOR) the main performance metric and record handle acceleration on every impact. One rig then gives both a performance map and a feel map, with modal analysis and COP as reference measurements that explain them.

Measurement methods, standards and precedents

No published rig found here makes an automated 2D map of a flat face with a real ball. The closest are a robot padel rig (one line, vibration) and a manual cricket bat grid (2D, rebound).

Standards to build the protocol on

  • NCAA BBCOR protocol (2009, 2024), based on ASTM F2219-23. It uses an air cannon, a light-trap speed gate accurate to 1.5 mph, and a bat on a pivot. Inbound speed is 136 mph, within ±2 mph (2009) or ±1 mph (2024). Each location is the average of 6 valid impacts, stepped in ½ in increments to the peak, and a calibrated reference bat checks the rig over time. This is the best template for the project's testing protocol.
  • ASTM F2398-11(2024) measures a bat's moment of inertia and COP by swinging it on a pivot.
  • USGA/R&A TPX3004 is a pendulum contact-time test at several points on a golf clubface, a precedent for mapping a face.

Other hurling sources. Fahey et al. 1998 covers ash hurley properties, sliotar test proposals and a hand-held pivot point; read the full text before saying it covers the sweet spot. Alsakarneh, Quinn et al. 2012 models sliotar COR. King & Celmer 2017 is a conference abstract: a preliminary attempt to define the sweet spot of one 34 in ash hurley.

Scanning laser Doppler vibrometry gives non-contact modal maps with no added sensor mass (Rothberg et al. 2017 review).

Rig architecture options

The usual front-runner is a fixed, calibrated impactor with the implement moved under it on an XY stage. Motion and fast data capture run on separate hardware, and one Python script drives both.

The hurley rides on the stage, so the impactor and speed sensors never move and stay calibrated. Each arrow is one step of the per-point loop the script runs (OB4).

Positioning (OB2)

  • Move the implement, not the impactor. This keeps the launcher and speed sensors aligned and calibrated. The catch is that the carriage becomes part of the boundary condition, so mount the hurley through a defined pivot or elastic support.
  • Drives. Belts (e.g. GT2) are fast, cheap and suit long travel, but are less stiff. Lead or ball screws are precise and hold position unpowered, but are slower.
  • Curved faces. A curved bás may need a Z or tilt axis to keep impacts normal to the surface.
  • Controller. GRBL is open-source G-code firmware for an Arduino Uno or Nano, with step pulses up to 30 kHz, driven from Python over USB. Fit homing limit switches so every run starts from a known zero.

Boundary condition

  • Free-free on elastic is the most repeatable and the standard for modal testing; Curtis et al. argue it is closer to hand-held play than rigid clamping.
  • Pivot or handle clamp: BBCOR uses vee clamps with compliant grip material and the knob 6 in from the pivot; Blanes clamped at 2 Nm. Write the clamp torque into the spec.
  • Simulated grip is the most realistic but the least repeatable. Rebound maps are fairly insensitive to support; vibration maps are not.

DAQ and software (OB3–OB4)

  • Sequence. One script loops: move, settle, arm the DAQ, fire, acquire, save, check, next point.
  • Sample rate. Bat–ball contact lasts over 0.5 ms (Nathan 2003), and an impact of duration T excites modes up to about 1.5/T (Cross 2004). Roughly 20–50 kS/s per channel with anti-alias filtering is a working estimate, not a standard; Blanes used 30 kHz.
  • Triggering. The nidaqmx Python library supports reference triggers with pretrigger samples, so a light gate or the accelerometer can start a capture without losing the event's start. Check whether a multiplexed DAQ quotes its rate per channel or shared.

Loughborough resources and safety approvals

The STI is part of the Wolfson School and already owns most of the expensive kit this rig needs, so borrowing could shrink OB1 a lot. Whether MEng students can use it, and on what terms, is not on the website, so ask.

  • Sports Technology Institute. A £15M facility with about 50 academics, researchers, technicians and PhD students (About). Its facilities page lists Photron SA1 and Fastcam high-speed cameras, ball launchers and cannons, and golf and kicking robots. It also lists Vicon and Coda motion capture, Kistler force plates, Instron machines, DataPhysics and M+P DAQ, and a machine shop.
  • Wolfson School facilities. Machine tool and electronics workshops, undergraduate workshops with technician support, vibration analysis labs, and STEMLab for drop-in rapid prototyping.
  • Equipment database. The full university catalogue shows after sign-in; use it to find cameras, sensors and DAQ to borrow.

Safety approvals. Start these early, especially for an air cannon:

  • Loughborough's Pressure System Safety Policy covers gas above 0.5 bar gauge. Every system needs a risk assessment approved by an authorised person, plus registration.
  • Above 250 bar·L (pressure × volume), a competent person must also draw up a Written Scheme of Examination. A reservoir of 10 L at 8 bar is 80 bar·L, which stays under that threshold.
  • Pendulum and projectile rigs need guarding, an enclosure (e.g. polycarbonate), interlocks and an e-stop, and the gantry adds pinch points.

Getting started: the first six weeks

The kickoff decisions gate everything else, so get them in week 1. The sweet spot metric picks the impactor and sensors, so concept work waits on it.

Safety approvals and the specimen request start at once because they have the longest lead times. The software can start in week 2 on dummy data, since the move–fire–capture loop is the same whichever impactor wins. The pilot modal test gives real mode frequencies to fix the sample rate, and an early map for the interim report.

Questions for Torpey and supervisors

The first four questions decide the rig's architecture, so take them to the kickoff meeting.

Scope and definition

  • Which sweet spot definition matters to Torpey: ball exit speed (ACOR), handle vibration ("feel"), COP, or a combined map? What method sits behind the "increased sweet spot" claim?
  • Which products come first (Bambú Speed, Strike, Sleek, Goalkeeper, Ash 6-Star)? Which other sports does "a range of equipment" mean?
  • What impact speeds are representative? Is a game-speed cannon allowed, or is a lower-speed comparative test acceptable?
  • Is a full 2D map across the bás needed, or a scan along the centreline?
  • How should the hurley be held: free, pivoted or clamped? Is there a Torpey or GAA reference condition?
  • Should hurleys be tested with or without metal bands on the bás, and with grips fitted?
  • Which sliotar: Torpey's own or another brand? Is Torpey's ball GAA-compliant?

Existing knowledge

  • Can Torpey share the STI Bambú test reports, or its in-house methods and data (impact speeds, support, ball brand)?
  • What test equipment does Torpey already have: cannon, light gates, high-speed camera, accelerometers, impact hammer?

Resources and logistics

  • What budget and lead times apply to manufacture, DAQ, sensors and specimens? What does "appropriately requested" manufacture involve (who machines it, which approval route)?
  • Can the project use STI cameras and launchers, and how does an MEng student get access?
  • Where will the rig be built and where will it live afterwards: Loughborough, Sixmilebridge, or both? Must it be transportable?
  • What repeatability and accuracy targets would Torpey accept (CV of the rebound ratio, location tolerance, repeats per point)?
  • Are there confidentiality or IP limits on publishing maps and product comparisons in the project report?

Common pitfalls

Most of these rigs fail on repeatability, not on design, so build the checks into the protocol from day one.

  • Undefined sweet spot. If the metric is not stated, maps cannot be compared across products or sessions.
  • Impact speed drift. Cannon pressure, ball wear and temperature all shift speed. Measure every shot, reject or normalise out-of-tolerance ones, and condition the balls.
  • Commanded vs actual impact point. Check the real hit location with the camera or a witness mark; a curved face also gives oblique impacts.
  • Support sensitivity. Vibration metrics change with the clamp, so fix the clamp torque and the carriage mass.
  • Low-speed maps. Slow or long-contact impacts excite fewer modes, so a low-speed map may not match game speed. Bower's 2012 result is explicitly for low-speed impacts.
  • Material variability and damage. Wood varies between and within trees, changes with moisture, and accumulates damage under repeated hits. Record mass, moisture and mode frequencies, randomise the point order, and re-hit a reference point to detect drift.
  • Triggering. Watch for false triggers from gantry vibration, a missing pretrigger buffer, and no settle time after a move.
  • Throughput. 60 points × 6 repeats is 360 impacts per hurley, so automatic ball reload is often the real bottleneck.
  • Aliasing. Too low a sample rate, or no anti-alias filter, corrupts the vibration data.
